Recycled polyester shows up on a lot of hang-tags now, usually next to a leaf icon and a line about plastic bottles. The implication is simple: buying this garment is the responsible version of buying a polyester garment. The research doesn't back that up.

## What the label actually means

"Recycled polyester" almost always means the fibre was made from recycled PET plastic bottles — not from old clothes. That's a real, if narrow, benefit: it uses fewer freshly-made petrochemicals to make the fibre. It is also, structurally, still polyester: a synthetic, petroleum-based polymer. Recycling it doesn't change what it's made of or how it behaves once it's a garment on your back.

## What a recent lab study actually found

In <time datetime="2025-12">December 2025</time>, the Changing Markets Foundation published lab results from testing a batch of garments — a mix of cotton, virgin polyester and recycled polyester — bought from five major global fashion brands. The testing, carried out by a university microplastics research group, measured how many fibres each garment shed when washed.

The result ran against the marketing: recycled polyester released roughly 55 percent more microfibres on average than virgin polyester, and the fibres it shed were smaller — which makes them travel further and penetrate deeper into tissue once released. Recycling the plastic didn't make the fabric shed less; by this measure, it shed more.

## Why would recycling make it shed *more*?

The study doesn't fully resolve this, but points to how the recycled fibre itself is made: breaking down and re-spinning PET can leave the resulting fibre more brittle and prone to fragmenting under the mechanical stress of a wash cycle. The headline point stands regardless of the exact mechanism — "recycled" describes where the raw material came from, not how durable or low-shedding the finished fibre is.

## The part that matters more than the percentage

The study's own conclusion isn't "avoid recycled, buy virgin instead." It's closer to the opposite: optimising which *kind* of polyester you buy can't fix a problem that comes from the fibre type itself. No amount of better weaving, tighter yarns or washing-machine filters fully stops a plastic fibre from shedding plastic. The fix the researchers point to is using less synthetic fibre overall — not picking the better-marketed synthetic.

## What this means when you're actually shopping

- A "made from recycled bottles" claim tells you about the fibre's origin, not its environmental performance once it's clothing.
- It is not evidence the garment sheds less microplastic — the opposite was measured, at least in this study.
- The same research found some garments described as "recycled" online whose physical care labels did not confirm it — so the claim on a product page and the label sewn into the garment can disagree.
- None of this makes a polyester garment unwearable. It does mean the "recycled equals responsible" shortcut doesn't hold up, and a fibre choice is worth checking on its own terms rather than taking the hang-tag's word for it.

That's the whole point of checking the fibre, not the marketing copy: some claims hold up, and some don't survive contact with the actual study.
