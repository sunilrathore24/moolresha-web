You're in a shop, or scrolling a product page. The tag says **100% Linen**. Feels like enough to go on, right?

It isn't — not because the label lies, but because it only answers one question. "100% Linen" tells you what the fabric is *made of*. It tells you almost nothing about how the shirt will feel, hang, breathe, or age. Two shirts can both be 100% linen and be completely different garments.

Here's what to actually check.

## The short answer

"100% Linen" confirms the **fibre**: it's all flax, no cotton or polyester blended in. That's genuinely useful to know. But the qualities you care about in a shirt — the coolness, the drape, the softness, whether you can see through it — come from the **weight, the weave, the yarn, and the finish**. Check those, and you can tell a good linen shirt from a mediocre one regardless of what the tag boasts.

## What "100% Linen" actually tells you

Linen is woven from the fibres of the **flax plant** (*Linum usitatissimum*) — one of the oldest textile materials we know of. As a fibre, flax is strong, absorbent, and breathable, and it dries faster than cotton, which is exactly why linen feels good in hot, humid weather.

So "100% Linen" is a real signal. It means:

- The fabric is entirely flax — no hidden synthetic blend.
- You can expect linen's natural behaviour: breathable, moisture-friendly, quick-drying, and prone to a relaxed wrinkle.

That's the ceiling of what the composition label promises.

## What it does NOT tell you

This is the part most shoppers miss. "100% Linen" is silent on every one of these:

- **Fabric weight** — a gauzy summer linen and a structured jacket-weight linen can carry the identical tag.
- **Weave** — how tightly and in what pattern the yarns are interlaced, which drives opacity and drape.
- **Yarn fineness** — whether the flax was spun into a fine, smooth yarn or a coarse, slubby one.
- **Finishing** — whether it's been washed/softened, and how it'll feel against your skin on day one.
- **Construction** — stitching and make quality, which no fibre label addresses.

None of these appear in the two words on the tag. You have to check them yourself.

## The four things to actually check

### 1. Weight — feel it, and hold it to the light

Fabric weight is often given as **GSM** (grams per square metre) — literally how much a square metre of the cloth weighs. Higher GSM is heavier; lower GSM is lighter. But here's the key: **weight is a trade-off, not a quality score.**

- **Lighter linen** — cooler, more breathable, more likely to be slightly sheer. Great for hot-weather shirts.
- **Heavier linen** — more structure, more opacity, more substantial feel. Better where you want the shirt to hold its shape.

If there's no GSM listed, hold the fabric up to a light. A lot of light through it means a light, airy (possibly see-through) weave; less light means a denser cloth. Neither is "better" — it depends on what you want the shirt to do.

### 2. Weave — read it in the light

Weight isn't the whole story, because **weave density and yarn structure also change how opaque and how substantial** a fabric feels. A loosely woven light linen drapes softly and breathes freely; a tighter weave feels crisper and more structured. Tilt the fabric to the light and look at how open the weave is. Match it to your use: airy and relaxed, or crisp and sharp.

### 3. Yarn fineness — the source of "why does this feel nicer?"

Linen yarn fineness is traditionally described by **Lea** (the linen equivalent of the count systems used for other fibres). A finer yarn generally gives a smoother, softer-feeling cloth; a coarser yarn gives that characteristic rustic, slubby linen texture. One isn't superior — a fine sheeting linen and a textured rustic linen are just different pleasures. But if two shirts feel very different at the same weight, the yarn is usually why.

### 4. Finish and feel — trust your hand

Run the fabric between your fingers. Some linen is sold crisp and raw; some is pre-washed or garment-washed to be soft from the first wear. There's no wrong answer, but know which you're getting — crisp linen softens with washing over time, so a stiff shirt today may become your favourite in a month.

## About the wrinkles

Linen creases. That's the fibre's nature — flexing the fabric stresses its structure and it holds the fold. This is **not a flaw**, and it's not a sign of cheap linen. If a relaxed, lived-in look isn't for you, linen may simply not be your fibre — but no amount of money buys a 100% linen shirt that won't wrinkle.

## How to compare two linen shirts

Put two "100% Linen" shirts side by side and you now have a checklist:

1. **Weight** — which suits your climate and use? (Light for heat, heavier for structure.)
2. **Weave** — hold both to the light. How opaque, how open?
3. **Yarn/feel** — which hand-feel do you prefer, smooth or textured?
4. **Finish** — crisp-and-will-soften, or already soft?
5. **Make** — even stitching, secure seams, buttons that feel solid.

The tag is identical. Everything that matters is in your hands.

## The honest bottom line

"100% Linen" is a good thing to see — it means you're getting real flax and its genuine hot-weather comfort. But treat it as the first question, not the last. Weight, weave, yarn and finish are what separate a linen shirt you'll love from one you'll leave in the wardrobe. Now you know what to look for.
